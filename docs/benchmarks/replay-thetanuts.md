## The answer first

- **The vault charged 0 for every mint.** The attacker flash-borrowed 153,054,600,569 of the vault's 153,054,600,572 index tokens (all but 3) and redeemed them with one `claim`. That left a supply of 3 and 1 raw unit of each of the five components. Then 37 `mint` calls created 153,192,349,709 index tokens, enough to repay the loan and its fee. Each mint charged `stored × amount / totalSupply` of each component, floored: 1 × 2 / 3, then 1 × 4 / 5, then 1 × 2^k / (2^k + 1), and finally 1 × 15,753,396,239 / 137,438,953,473. Every quotient is below 1, so all 185 component transfers were for 0.
- **The claim was not inflated.** It paid floor(stored × 153,054,600,569 / 153,054,600,572) of each component, at or below the exact pro-rata share. The loss is the mints. The vault gave away its whole backing in exchange for index tokens it then recreated for nothing.
- **The class is rounding direction, not precision.** The exact first charge is 2/3 of a raw unit. FP127 carries it to 38.53 digits and FP19 to 71.65, but a token transfer moves whole raw units, so a rule still has to pick one. Floor gives 0, which is the attack unchanged. Rounding up gives 1. Rounded up, the charge never lets the stored-to-supply ratio fall below 1/3, so any sequence that mints 153,192,349,709 tokens must pay at least 51,064,116,570 raw units of every component. The claim took at most 49,716,431,047 of any one, so the attack loses on all five. With the attacker's own amounts, rounding up costs 76,596,174,855 per component.
- **Rounding to nearest is not the fix either.** It blocks the attacker's own sequence (2/3 rounds to 1). But an attacker who keeps each mint below half the supply mints for free every time and reaches 153,192,349,709 tokens in 63 mints instead of 37.

Reading the table: FP127 and FP19 compute the 2/3 almost exactly, with at least 38 and 71 correct digits. That does not decide anything, because the vault moves ERC-20 raw units and 2/3 of a unit has to become 0 or 1. Floor, the deployed choice, rounds in the minter's favour. Ceil rounds in the vault's favour and closes the attack whatever the arithmetic in front of it. Rounding to nearest only looks like a fix when the attacker's amounts were tuned to floor.

## Round up: what any mint sequence costs

Write s for a component's stored amount and S for the supply. A rounded-up charge c on a mint of a satisfies c ≥ s·a/S, so (s + c)/(S + a) ≥ s/S: the ratio s/S never falls. After the claim it is 1/3 for every component. To mint the 153,192,349,709 tokens the attacker needed, the supply ends at 153,192,349,712, so s ends at least (153,192,349,709 + 3)/3 and the attacker pays at least 51,064,116,570 raw units of each component. This holds for any amounts in any order. `test_round_up_costs_more_than_the_claim_took` asserts the invariant after every mint and both bounds against each component's take.

## The bytecode evidence

Both `claim` (selector `0x379607f5`) and `mint` (`0xa0712d68`) compute a component's share by jumping into the same two compiler helpers: solc 0.8.9's checked multiply (entry 12158) and checked divide (entry 12189). The opcode trace records 190 executions: 5 in the claim and 185 in the mints, five per call, one per component. In each one:

- pc **12165**: `DIV` of 2^256 − 1 by the stored amount, the overflow check before the product;
- pc **12186**: `MUL` stored × amount;
- pc **12220**: `DIV` of that product by totalSupply. EVM `DIV` floors, and nothing adds a remainder back.

## Where this differs from the reports

- "A vulnerability in the vault's redemption logic", with "`backing * amount / totalSupply`" letting the attacker "withdraw more funds than they were entitled to" ([Cryptopolitan](https://www.cryptopolitan.com/hack-deprecated-thetanuts-vault/), citing ExVul); "allowed inflated payouts" ([Cryip](https://cryip.co/?p=12478)). The redemption, `claim`, paid floor(stored × 153,054,600,569 / 153,054,600,572) of each component. For the BTC put that is 49,716,431,047 against an exact 49716431047.02552, in the vault's favour. The formula is right, and so is its rounding on the way out. The same formula is wrong on the way in: `mint` uses it, floored, for what the minter pays.
- "Repeated mint-and-redeem cycles" ([Cryip](https://cryip.co/?p=12478), [Cryptopolitan](https://www.cryptopolitan.com/hack-deprecated-thetanuts-vault/)). There is one `claim` and then 37 `mint` calls. Nothing is redeemed between the mints. The flash loan supplied the index tokens for the one claim; it did not amplify a loop.

## Caveat

- **Bytecode, not source.** The function names (`claim`, `mint`, `initWithdraw`) come from 4-byte selectors. The meanings of the storage fields come from how this transaction reads and writes them. The model matches every executed instruction that matters here, but other paths through the contract (owner functions, other branches) are not replayed.
- **Counterfactuals start from the chain's post-claim state.** Under a vault that rounded mints up, the attacker would not have claimed in the first place. The round-up bound shows that no mint sequence after this claim pays.
- **Not confirmed here.** The press puts the loss at about $2.1 million and says a whitehat recovered most of it. This transaction realised 105,471.499078 USDC. The rest left as three component-vault tokens, and their vaults held no USDC at the block before (`components[].usdcHeldBefore`), so this replay does not price them. Nothing in the transaction shows a recovery.
