## What it is

FP127 is a signed 127.128 fixed-point library for the EVM: one `int256` per value, 128 fractional bits, 53 operations including `exp`, `ln`, `sqrt`, `pow`, an exact integer power, the error function and the normal distribution, both real branches of the Lambert W function and π as a constant, written as one Yul source that compiles to a deployed contract and to an inlinable Solidity library. It is validated against mpmath at 200 decimal places, and it is deployed on Sepolia at `0xb349DB36D51b0BC10Dd43aF70009b7B6E7c1CBe4` (v6, Blockulator 2.2).

FP256 is its companion for everything outside the money range: a 256-bit binary float in the same `int256`, 237 significant bits (about 71 digits) at any magnitude from 10<sup>−78,913</sup> to 10<sup>78,913</sup>, 65 operations, every one correctly rounded (0 misrounded in 360,590 random inputs). It comes from the same generator in the same two forms, and FP256 v5 (Blockulator 2.2) is deployed on Sepolia as four objects, one idea each ([below](#the-second-ruler)).

The one number that matters: $10,000 at 10% APR compounded daily for a year, one multiplication per day. After 365 steps ABDK is right to 17 digits, Solady, PRBMath and Balancer to 16, ABDKMathQuad to 31, Maker's rpow to 24, FP127 to 36 [compound, N = 365]. The [ladder](/results/ladder/) has thirteen of these scenarios; for Blockulator 2.2, 9,408 of its 9,570 cells were re-run against the chain, and the rest need more gas than a public RPC allows.

## Why WAD loses digits

The full argument is on the [why](/why/) page. The short form: a WAD library has about 60 bits of fraction and 196 bits of overflow headroom that you cannot store a value in, every `mulWad` rounds once, and a protocol does not call it once. The slopes of every library's digit loss against N are the same, about one digit per decade. The intercepts are twenty digits apart, and the intercept is what you keep.

## The Huff era

The first FP127 was pure Huff. I wrote it that way because I wanted to count gas at the opcode level, and because at the time I thought the right shape was one contract at one address that every protocol `staticcall`s. It worked. It reached 34 operations, it was presented at EthCC[9] in Cannes, and the archived bytecode is still on Sepolia at `0xfae694D0c2c44181791F838c54Ed64C3151FfE30`, where the current test suites compare against it on every fuzzed input.

It taught me three things. First, almost nobody will audit Huff. The pool of people who can read a dispatcher built from `dup1 __FUNC_SIG eq jumpi` chains is tiny, and a math library that cannot be audited is a liability no matter how good the numbers are. Second, `huffc` has an internal size limit on the expanded source. The combined macros hit it the day I added a second Lambert W branch, and `lambertWm1`, which also never worked, never shipped. Third, a `staticcall` to the Huff object adds about 1,200 gas to every `add` and `mul`, which is fine for a price calculation and rough in a hot loop. I asked two questions about this in a post I never got to publish: Huff versus Yul, and what the trust model should be. Both are answered below.

## The port

The current FP127 is one file, `FP127.yul.src`, holding Yul function bodies and nothing else. A generator reads it and emits two outputs. The deployed object wraps the bodies in a selector switch. The library, `FP127Lib.sol`, puts each body and the helpers it calls inside a `memory-safe` assembly block, one `internal pure` function per op. The suggestion to ship both forms came from Austin Griffith and Hadrien Croubois, and it dissolves the choice I was stuck on: the object gives you one address that every contract on a chain agrees with by construction, and the library gives you the same math inlined for the loops where the call overhead matters.

They agree because they are the same text. The equivalence suites then fuzz it across the full `int256` domain: the object and the library return identical results, reverts and revert data included, and match the Huff bytecode on Sepolia bit for bit on the arithmetic and within 2<sup>−100</sup> on the transcendentals, which now round where the Huff floored, wherever the Huff computed a defined answer. The port also made arithmetic checked. Where the Huff wrapped silently on overflow, the Yul reverts with `Overflow()`, `DivisionByZero()` or `OutOfRange()`. Division, which the old documentation described as a 62-bit shortcut, turned out to have been a full 512-by-256 `mulDiv` all along; the docs were stale, and the equivalence suite is what caught it.

Precision per op is in [`precision.json`](/data/precision.json): `add`, `sub`, the comparisons and the integer ops (`floor`, `ceil`, `round`, `factorial`) exact; `mul`, `div` and `sqrt` within one ULP, rounded down (`div` toward zero); every transcendental, `exp`, `ln`, `pow`, `cbrt`, the error function and both Lambert W branches among them, the nearest word at every committed vector. The ladder forced that. Under the Huff's floor-everywhere policy `exp(ln(x))` came back 8 ULPs low on every call and the error marched in a straight line; v2 computed with 64 guard bits and rounded once, and the round trip came back the same word every time; v5 rounds every transcendental to the nearest word, and v6 keeps every word for less gas. The runtime is 21,858 bytes of the 24,576 allowed, and `lambertWm1`, the routine the Huff compiler could not fit, is in it.

## Trust model

The object has no storage, no owner, no proxy and no upgrade path. It was deployed with CREATE2 through the deterministic proxy at `0x4e59b44847b379578588920cA78FbF26c0B4956C` with the salt `"FP127 v6"`, so the same init code lands at the same address on every chain. It is verified on Sourcify, `match` on creation and runtime bytecode. A change to the source is a new init code hash, a new salt string, a new address and a new row in the deployment record. There is nothing to freeze after an audit because there is nothing that can move. That was the answer to my second question: not a multisig, not a registry, just an address you can recompute from the repository.

## The edges

The ladder measures a slope. The [edges](/results/edges/) measure the cliffs: 139 probes, one call each at the end of a domain, through ten libraries, every cell either with its digits or with how the library had no answer: zero below its floor, a revert, an argument outside its word, or no way to compute the op [edges.json]. ln(1 + 10<sup>−9</sup>), a per-second rate inside a logarithm, has 8 to 9 digits on an 18-decimal grid and 30 on FP127; exp(−42), a probability of one in 10<sup>18</sup>, is zero on the grid and 21 digits here; 10<sup>−10</sup> × 10<sup>−10</sup> is zero on every grid but this one, Quad's and FP256's; N(−9), the normal tail an option lives in, is zero on WAD and 21 digits here. And FP127's own walls are on the page: exp(−89) is zero, exp(89) reverts, 1.0001<sup>887272</sup> is one bit past the word, a root of 10<sup>−30</sup> keeps eight digits. ABDKMathQuad, binary128, keeps 29 to 34 wherever the argument is not near one or in the normal tail, because its point moves; it also keeps four fewer digits than FP127 in the middle and rounds at every op, so it loses on every ladder but the option walk at N = 10,000 and annual compounding past FP127's top.

The accrual maths of the protocols most of the money sits in are in the same tables, judged against the model each one states: Aave's and Morpho's three-term expansion of e<sup>rt</sup> keeps 6 digits after one idle year and 1 after twenty-seven [accrual-continuous, N = 365 and 10,000]; Maker's rpow keeps 23 after ten thousand days [compound, N = 10,000]; Morpho's per-second rate in eighteen decimals has ten digits before any arithmetic. None of these are mistakes; each is right for the interval it was built for.

## What it is not for

Range, and the floor. FP127 tops out near 1.7 × 10<sup>38</sup> and its grid stops at 2<sup>−128</sup>. That is every balance a protocol will ever hold in whole units, but it is not every raw `uint256` in wei: a token with 18 decimals and a 10<sup>21</sup> supply is at 10<sup>39</sup> in wei. FP127 is for the maths in the middle, the rate, the price, the ratio, the exponent, and the conversion back to wei is one `toFixed18` at the edge. It is not a replacement for the integer arithmetic of a transfer, it is not for ERC20 balances, and it is not cheap enough to put in a path that does not need the digits. If your protocol multiplies once and settles, Solady is cheaper and good enough. If it accrues, iterates, chains transcendentals or subtracts two large numbers that are nearly equal, the [ladder](/results/ladder/) is where you find out how many digits you have left.

## The second ruler

A fixed point spends 127 bits on a range nobody uses and still has walls; a float with half the word has walls only at 10<sup>±4,932</sup> and four fewer digits. The other half of that word was idle, so I filled it. FP256 is a 256-bit binary float: one sign bit, a 19-bit exponent, a 236-bit fraction with an implicit one, about 71 digits at any magnitude from 10<sup>−78,913</sup> to 10<sup>78,913</sup>, no NaN, no infinity, no subnormals. Every kernel in FP127 was already a significand engine (the integer power routine computes exactly that pair, a 256-bit mantissa and an exponent, before it rounds back to the grid), so FP256 v1 took the kernels as they were: the arithmetic, the root, the comparisons and the conversions were exact to the 237-bit grid at every vector, and `exp`, `ln` and `pow` held 146, 149 and 123 bits. v2 refit the kernels for the wide word, at 2<sup>−254</sup> with 512-bit products, and eighteen of the nineteen ops return the correctly rounded word at every committed vector, `pow` within one ULP. v3 (Blockulator 2.0) added nine ops. v4 (Blockulator 2.1) had 67, every one correctly rounded or exact. v5 (Blockulator 2.2), live on Sepolia since 2026-10-22, has 65 in four objects by idea (FP256, the number, at `0xAa1f71B7B08d568379b28E1488c6B737a1C6eb52`; FP256E, FP256H and FP256S for the elementary, hyperbolic and special functions). All are documented on the [FP256](/library/fp256/) page.

On the ladder it holds 67 to 72 digits where the arithmetic is exact (daily compounding, the bonding curve, the cumulative product), 62 to 73 where exp or ln carry the step (per-second accrual, continuous accrual, the geometric mean, daily compounding by pow), exact on four of the round trip's ten inputs and 70 to 72 on the rest (exp(ln x) returns the same word at every N), and 66 on the option walk at d₁ = −12, where FP127 keeps 3 and WAD zero [deep-tail-option, reference, N = 10,000]. On the edges it has no zero, no revert and no range anywhere: 71 digits of 10^−10 × 10^−10 and of √10^−30, 1/10^−30 exactly, 71 to 72 of e^x from −100 to 136, 71 of ln(10^−30) and ln(10^38), 62 of ln(1 + 10^−9) and 62 to 63 of a per-second rate raised to a year of seconds through `pow` (71 through `compound`), which is what a 71-digit input leaves after the cancellation [edges]. Pi through its own primitives reaches 47 digits at six terms where Quad stops at 33 [pi.md]. The price is the moving point: `add` 662 gas inline against FP127's 124, `mul` 530 against 263, `div` 800 against 645, `ln` 3,006 against 600, `pow` 5,541 against 3,537, `sqrt` 1,205 against 1,025; `exp` is cheaper, 3,295 against 6,006 [gas256.json]. FP127 stays the ruler for the middle of the number line, where it has 38 digits at 1 to the float's 71 at a fifth to four fifths of the gas on `add`, `mul`, `div` and `ln`; FP256 is the ruler for everything the edges showed a fixed point cannot hold.
